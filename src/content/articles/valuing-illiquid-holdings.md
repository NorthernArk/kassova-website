Listed securities price themselves every day. The rental property, the interest in a private fund and the loan to a relative do not, and they are often a large share of a family's wealth. They are also where records go stale.

## Every valuation needs four things

1. **A date.** A value is true as of a moment.
2. **A basis.** An appraisal, a fund manager's statement, a recent transaction, cost, or an internal estimate.
3. **A source document.** The appraisal report or the statement itself, kept with the value.
4. **Who accepted it.** The person who recorded the value and the person who reviewed it.

Without these, a report mixes this morning's share prices with a property value from years ago and presents the total as current.

## Know the hierarchy of inputs

Accounting standards describe how reliable a valuation input is. IFRS 13 sets out a fair value hierarchy, from quoted prices in active markets, through other observable inputs, to unobservable inputs. Guidelines such as the IPEV Valuation Guidelines describe practice for private equity and venture capital interests. You do not need to apply them formally to benefit from the idea: say which kind of input each value rests on.

## Show age, not just amount

A report on holdings should show each value's date and basis next to it, and flag values older than your policy allows. A family that sees "valued at cost, 2019" next to a holding asks the right question.

## Link value to structure

Many holdings are owned through trusts or holding companies. When ownership is recorded as a structure, a valuation change rolls up to each owner's share without a separate spreadsheet.
