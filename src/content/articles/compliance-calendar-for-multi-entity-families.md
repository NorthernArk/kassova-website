Each entity a family holds brings its own obligations: returns, estimated payments, annual reports, renewals and the conditions in loan and partnership agreements. One missed date can cost more than a year of careful bookkeeping saves.

## Where the dates come from

- **Federal tax.** The IRS publishes its tax calendars in Publication 509, with due dates for returns, estimated payments and employment taxes. Dates move when they fall on a weekend or holiday, so use the current year's edition.
- **States.** Annual reports, franchise taxes and registered-agent renewals vary by state and entity type.
- **Agreements.** Loan covenants, reporting duties to lenders and partnership notice periods are dates too, and the ones most often forgotten.
- **Insurance and property.** Renewals, assessments and inspections.

## What makes a calendar work

A list of dates is not enough. Each line needs:

1. the entity it belongs to;
2. the obligation, in plain words;
3. the due date and the date work must start;
4. one named owner;
5. a record of when it was done, with the evidence.

Dates without owners get missed by everyone at once.

## Start with the costly ones

Begin with the obligations that carry penalties or interest when late: tax returns and estimated payments. The IRS explains that estimated tax is generally paid in installments through the year, and underpaying can bring a penalty. Then add state filings, then agreement dates.
