In a family's structure, entities constantly owe each other: a holding company lends to an operating company, a trust pays an expense for a rental, management fees move between them. Each transaction appears twice, once in each entity's books. If the two sides disagree, the consolidation will not tie out.

## Record both sides at once

The most reliable way to keep intercompany balances in agreement is to record both sides from one action. A loan from A to B creates a receivable in A and a payable in B, with the same amount, date and reference. When each side is typed separately, by different people or weeks apart, differences are guaranteed.

## Match before you consolidate

At each close, list every intercompany pair and compare the balances:

1. A's receivable from B against B's payable to A.
2. Differences get an owner and a reason: timing, a missing entry, or a wrong amount.
3. Clear them in the period, not at year end.

Consolidation standards such as IFRS 10 require intragroup balances and transactions to be eliminated in full when preparing consolidated statements. Elimination only works cleanly when the two sides already match.

## Agree the terms

Intercompany loans and fees should have terms: interest, repayment and the basis of any fee. Tax authorities expect transactions between related parties to be priced as if between independent parties; the IRS describes this under transfer pricing. Written terms also make the entries easy to check.

## Let the structure do the work

When ownership is recorded as a structure, the system knows which entities are related and which balances must be eliminated, so the consolidated view is built from the books rather than rebuilt in a spreadsheet.
