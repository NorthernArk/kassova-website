"Keep everything forever" feels safe and is not a policy. It makes the records that matter harder to find. A retention policy says what each kind of record is, how long it is kept and who decides when it goes.

## What the IRS says

For United States federal tax purposes, the IRS ties retention to the period of limitations, the time in which a return can be amended or tax assessed. Its guidance includes these periods:

- generally, keep records for **3 years** from the date the return was filed;
- **3 years** if you file a claim for credit or refund after filing, from the filing date or **2 years** from when the tax was paid, whichever is later;
- **7 years** if you file a claim for a loss from worthless securities or a bad debt deduction;
- **6 years** if you do not report income that you should have and it is more than 25% of the gross income shown on the return;
- **indefinitely** if you do not file a return, or file a fraudulent one;
- at least **4 years** after the tax becomes due or is paid, whichever is later, for employment tax records.

The IRS also notes that records connected to property should be kept until the period of limitations expires for the year in which you dispose of the property. For a family office holding property and private investments for decades, that can be a long time.

## Applying it to many entities

Each entity files its own returns, so each has its own clock. A practical policy:

1. Classify records: statements, invoices, agreements, tax returns, property and investment records, payroll.
2. Give each class a retention rule tied to an event (filing date, disposal date), not a fixed calendar date.
3. File each record against the entity it belongs to, so its clock is known.
4. Make disposal a reviewed step with a record of what was disposed of and who approved it.

Other rules, from states, other countries, lenders or insurers, may require longer periods. The IRS periods are a floor for federal tax, not a complete answer.
