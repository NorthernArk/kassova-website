Closing one company's books is a routine. Closing twenty related entities is a coordination problem: the same steps, in the same order, with dependencies between entities that owe each other money.

## The order of work

1. **Collect the sources.** Bank and card statements for each account of each entity, plus invoices, bills and payroll reports. Note what is missing before anything else; a close built on a missing statement has to be redone.
2. **Reconcile cash.** Each bank account's ledger balance against its statement. Unreconciled items get an owner and a note, not a plug.
3. **Record the period's entries.** Accruals, prepayments, depreciation and recurring charges.
4. **Match intercompany.** What one entity says another owes it should equal what the other says it owes. Differences are cleared before consolidation, not after.
5. **Review and approve.** A second person reviews entries above your threshold and every manual journal.
6. **Lock the period.** Once approved, the period is closed so that late changes need a deliberate reopening.
7. **Report and ask.** Produce the reports, and turn what needs the owner into a short list of decisions.

## Evidence at every step

The IRS asks businesses to keep records that support the income, deductions and credits shown on a return, and lists bank statements, invoices and receipts among them. A good close leaves that evidence attached to the figures it supports, so the question "where did this come from" has an answer in one step.

## Running it across clients

When you close for several clients, the checklist is the same but the state is not. A board with one row per client and one column per step shows, at a glance, who is waiting on a statement, who is waiting on an approval and who is done. That view, not a longer checklist, is what keeps a twenty-entity close from slipping.
